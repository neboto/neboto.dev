I added charts to neboto for the looks. They'd make a good demo, a nice screenshot for the website, and I didn't expect to use them much myself. It turned out I was wrong: they solve real problems, and they've become one of the best ways I know to build a relationship with an app.

## The Fargate proxy that wasn't undersized

One day I was having trouble with a Fargate service - a small proxy for private network access. It wasn't always needed, so it was sized as small as possible to save costs, and I shared it with a colleague. When it seemed to stop working, I assumed I'd undersized it and reached straight for the Terraform code to bump the CPU and memory.

I made the change quickly, but before opening a PR, the doubts started to creep in. I shouldn't be changing things I didn't fully understand.

neboto, as usual, was running in another tmux pane. I jumped over, switched to the right account with <kbd>P</kbd>, and searched for the service with <kbd>/</kbd> and `@ecs proxy`.

The service had one running task. I jumped to the task and tailed its logs with <kbd>t</kbd>. Nothing. I jumped back to the service with <kbd>Ctrl-O</kbd> and paused - it was time to look at CPU and memory.

I hit <kbd>m</kbd> and, to my surprise, the service had been pretty idle for the past hour. Widening the range to 24 hours with <kbd>3</kbd> told the same story: steady, low load.

So it wasn't undersized after all. I asked my colleague, and theirs was working fine, which pointed at my machine rather than the service. Troubleshooting locally, I found several defunct AWS SSM sessions left over in various panes. Once I'd terminated them, my local connection to the proxy worked again.

## Muscle memory

That was the moment <kbd>m</kbd> went into my muscle memory. When I'm jumping around services now, I hit it constantly for a quick glance at what's happening right now, or what's been happening over the past hour, day or week. Do that often enough and you build a relationship with a service and the app behind it - you learn what normal looks like and get a feel for its performance profile, so you notice when something isn't right.

From there the ideas started to flow. WAF, CloudFront, RDS - could I chart these in a way that gave real value?

## WAF and CloudFront

AWS's original WAF dashboard had a few simple charts and was quite usable. At some point it was reworked into something that sent you looking for an alternative (though a recent update seems to have improved it a lot). Either way, neboto's WAF view more than holds its own.

Allowed, blocked, counted, CAPTCHA and challenge requests share one chart, with the block rate underneath and the rules and labels in a table beside it. <kbd>Tab</kbd> into the table and <kbd>Enter</kbd> on a rule charts just that rule's actions, so you can see exactly which one started blocking and when. Then <kbd>x</kbd> opens that rule's sampled requests straight in your editor.

## RDS

RDS gets the same treatment. CPU, connections, read and write IOPS and latency, freeable memory and free storage each get a row with a sparkline and their last, min, max and average, so one glance tells you whether the database is busy, slow or running out of room. RDS events land on the chart as change markers too, so a reboot or failover lines up with whatever it did to the graph.

## Try it

Next time something looks off - or you just want a quick look at how a service is behaving - hit <kbd>m</kbd> before reaching for Terraform or opening the console. The [metrics guide](@/guide/lenses.md#metrics-m) covers the rest of the keys: statistics, longer ranges, every metric a resource has, and the change markers. If you haven't tried neboto yet, the [install guide](@/guide/install.md) will get you up and running in a minute.
